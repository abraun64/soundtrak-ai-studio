# Author facts — the generation half of Rule 10 (SYS-173)

Read by every drafter (thought-leadership, long-form, linkedin-post, Producer) before it writes a word in someone's voice. The sub-edit half is Rule 10 in `voice-rules.md` and its trace table; this half stops the fabrication being written at all.


The instruction "never fabricate autobiography" has existed since 2026-07-30 and drafts kept inventing it anyway (eds 22, 24, 26, 28). So this is a procedure that produces something, not a reminder.

**Before you draft — build the Author Facts list.** Read the operator-supplied source (brief, notes, library entry, interview transcript, quotes they gave) and copy out, verbatim or near-verbatim, every first-person fact it actually contains: things the author did, saw, ran, owned, tested, found, believed, felt, and every result or number tied to them. Note where each came from. This list is the ONLY place first-person material may come from. Your own earlier notes, a README or an edition brief that a model wrote are NOT operator source unless they quote the operator.

**While drafting — four things may only come from that list:**
1. a **scene** with the author in it (a meeting, a room, a moment with setting or reactions);
2. a **feeling, belief or realisation** of the author's ("for years I thought…", "I was wrong", "the point that stuck with me", "the lesson I took");
3. a claim about the author's **role, seniority, scope, employer or ownership** (including WHICH company something happened at);
4. a **result, finding, number or anecdote** attributed to the author or their team.

Keep each one at the strength the source gives it. Do not add duration ("for years"), struggle ("adoption crawled"), emphasis ("over and over") or an employer the source did not name, and do not swap the finding for a punchier one.

**When the piece wants something the list doesn't have**, do one of these, never the fourth option of making it up:
- state the point generally, without "I" ("Most rollouts lean on training first");
- use a clearly archetypal scene in second or third person ("Picture a quarterly review where…") that no reader would take as the author's memory;
- leave a visible placeholder for the operator: `[ASK: what actually happened when you rolled out X?]`. One honest gap beats one invented memory; the operator fills it in seconds.

**After drafting — append the Provenance Ledger** to your working notes (not the published copy): one row per item of the four types above that the draft contains.

| # | Draft line (short quote) | Type (scene / feeling / role / result) | Source (file + the words it rests on) |
|---|---|---|---|

A row whose source column would be empty means the line goes, before you hand the draft on. An archetypal scene gets `archetype` in the source column, and is allowed only when the line has no I, my, we or our in it. The sub-edit's Rule 10 pass checks this ledger independently against the source; it does not take it on trust.

**What this is worth, measured (SYS-173, Ed 28 held out).** The same Rule 10 trace was run on three drafts of the same piece from the same source. The original, drafted before Rule 10 existed, had 17 untraced or overstated author claims. A fresh draft under the rules as they stood, with Rule 10 already in the voice guide, had 11. Among them were the email test moved to Netwealth and a "we stopped relying on training" arc. A fresh draft that followed this procedure had 3, and it left one honest `[ASK: ...]` where the others had invented a story. That is one draft each, so read it as direction, not a benchmark. The sub-edit trace still runs on every draft, because a lower rate is not zero.
